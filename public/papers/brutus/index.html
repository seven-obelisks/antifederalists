Brutus

Brutus was the pseudonym used by an anonymous Anti-Federalist writer during the debates over ratification of the U.S. Constitution. The authorship is often attributed to Robert Yates, a New York judge and delegate to the Constitutional Convention.

Brutus’s essays warned that the proposed Constitution would create a strong central government that could overpower state authority. He expressed concern about the lack of a Bill of Rights and feared the judiciary would become too powerful, undermining individual freedoms. Brutus believed that a large republic would lead to the erosion of liberty and democratic principles.

Brutus I

Argues the proposed Constitution grants excessive power to the national government, particularly the legislature, and threatens the sovereignty of states and individual liberties.

Brutus II

Expresses concern that the Constitution’s provisions for a strong central government will erode state governments and centralize power, leading to potential tyranny.

Brutus III

Warns the Constitution’s necessary and proper clause gives the federal government too much flexibility, potentially allowing for overreach and the weakening of state authority.

Brutus IV

Critiques the proposed Senate for being too distant and undemocratic, arguing it would lead to a government controlled by elites, disconnected from the people’s needs.

Brutus IX

Warns that the Constitution’s centralization of military power will allow the federal government to use force against the states, undermining state sovereignty.

Brutus V

Expresses concern about the lack of a Bill of Rights in the Constitution, arguing that it leaves individuals vulnerable to government overreach and does not safeguard fundamental freedoms.

Brutus VI

Warns that the power of the federal judiciary under the Constitution would render state courts irrelevant and give the national government the ability to dominate legal matters.

Brutus VII

Argues the proposed system of representation will be ineffective, as the people’s voices will be drowned out by distant legislators, leading to a loss of accountability.

Brutus VIII

Critiques the structure of the executive branch, fearing it will give too much power to a single leader and lead to a de facto monarchy or tyrannical rule.

Brutus X

Argues that the size of the proposed republic under the Constitution will make effective representation impossible, leading to an oligarchical government controlled by a few elites.

Brutus XI

Expresses skepticism about the success of the proposed system of checks and balances, arguing that they will be insufficient to limit the growing power of the federal government.

Brutus XII

Warns that the proposed Senate will be an aristocratic body, disconnected from the people, and could lead to the creation of a permanent political elite.

Brutus XIII

Critiques the power of the federal government to regulate commerce, fearing it would dominate state economies and crush local industries.

Brutus XIV

Argues that the Constitution gives too much power to the executive, especially with regard to military command, leading to the risk of a military dictatorship.

Brutus XV

Expresses concern that the Constitution would lead to the dissolution of state governments and the creation of a centralized bureaucracy that would strip citizens of local representation.

Brutus XV

Calls for the rejection of the Constitution, arguing that its provisions give too much power to the central government and threaten the liberties of the people and the authority of the states.
