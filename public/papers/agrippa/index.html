Agrippa

Agrippa was the pseudonym used by an Anti-Federalist writer during the ratification debates of the U.S. Constitution. The authorship is most commonly attributed to James Winthrop, a Harvard-educated librarian and political thinker from Massachusetts.

Agrippa’s essays opposed the proposed Constitution, arguing that it would centralize power in a way that threatened state sovereignty and individual freedoms. He criticized the lack of a Bill of Rights and warned that the new government could easily become tyrannical, particularly through an unchecked judiciary. Agrippa believed explicit protections for civil liberties were essential to prevent the abuse of power.

Agrippa I

Warns that concentrating federal authority will erode local representation and threaten liberty.

Agrippa II

Argues that a large republic cannot effectively represent diverse communities or protect individual interests.

Agrippa III

Rejects the need for a new constitution, claiming that existing state structures are sufficient for governance.

Agrippa IV

Criticizes the proposed judiciary for granting the federal government dominance over state legal systems.

Agrippa V

Highlights the absence of a bill of rights as a major flaw, leaving civil liberties unprotected.

Agrippa VI

Describes how centralizing power weakens the independence and practical function of state governments.

Agrippa VII

Predicts unequal economic burdens under federal tax policies, especially in commercial states like Massachusetts.

Agrippa VIII

Raises concerns about federal control of trade enabling monopolies and disadvantaging local producers.

Agrippa IX

Flags vague constitutional language as a gateway for federal overreach and abuse of power.

Agrippa X

Calls for reforming the Articles of Confederation rather than adopting an untested central framework.

Agrippa XI

Urges rejection of the Constitution, warning that its adoption risks undermining both liberty and state autonomy.
