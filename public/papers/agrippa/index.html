Agrippa

Agrippa was the pseudonym used by an Anti-Federalist writer during the ratification debates of the U.S. Constitution. The authorship is most commonly attributed to James Winthrop, a Harvard-educated librarian and political thinker from Massachusetts.

Agrippa’s essays opposed the proposed Constitution, arguing that it would centralize power in a way that threatened state sovereignty and individual freedoms. He criticized the lack of a Bill of Rights and warned that the new government could easily become tyrannical, particularly through an unchecked judiciary. Agrippa believed explicit protections for civil liberties were essential to prevent the abuse of power.

Agrippa I

Argues that the proposed Constitution will centralize too much power in the national government, undermining the authority of states and threatening the protection of individual liberties.

Agrippa II

Warns that the size and scope of the new republic will prevent effective representation of the diverse interests of its citizens, weakening the ability to safeguard individual rights.

Agrippa III

Rejects the need for a new Constitution, asserting that the existing state structures are sufficient for governance and the proposed Constitution will create unnecessary and dangerous centralization.

Agrippa IV

Criticizes the proposed federal judiciary, which he believes will have too much power and diminish the role of state courts, leading to the erosion of state authority and the overreach of federal law.

Agrippa V

Expresses concern about the concentration of power in the hands of the executive, suggesting that the presidency could become a tool of tyranny under the proposed Constitution.

Agrippa VI

Warns that the lack of a Bill of Rights in the proposed Constitution leaves citizens vulnerable to government abuse and suggests that explicit protections for individual freedoms are essential.

Agrippa VII

Argues that the federal government’s powers over taxation, commerce, and the military are too expansive and could lead to oppressive control over the states and individuals.

Agrippa VIII

Concerns that the Senate, with its long terms and lack of rotation, will become disconnected from the people and form an aristocratic elite that will undermine democratic principles.

Agrippa IX

Fears that the Constitution’s provisions for a standing army and the president’s control over it will allow the national government to use military force to suppress dissent and assert unchecked power.

Agrippa X

Warns that the powers granted to the executive and federal judiciary could lead to a permanent ruling class that is unaccountable to the people, eroding republican values.

Agrippa XI

Reiterates concerns about the overreach of the federal government, warning that the new system could concentrate too much power at the national level and create a government that is too distant from the people to be effective.
