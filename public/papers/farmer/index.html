Federal Farmer

Federal Farmer was the pseudonym used by an anonymous Anti-Federalist writer during the U.S. Constitution ratification debates. The authorship is often attributed to Richard Henry Lee, a Virginia statesman.

Federal Farmer’s essays opposed the Constitution, arguing it would centralize power and weaken state authority. He criticized the lack of a Bill of Rights, fearing that without such protections, the new government could become tyrannical. Federal Farmer called for amendments to safeguard individual liberties and maintain the balance of power between the states and the federal government.

Farmer I

Warns that the new Constitution consolidates too much power at the national level, threatening state sovereignty and individual freedoms.

Farmer II

Argues the proposed government would be too distant and removed from the people, weakening representation and accountability.

Farmer III

Claims that the Constitution’s powers over taxation and the military are too expansive, leading to potential tyranny.

Farmer IV

Critiques the proposed Senate, arguing that its members will be detached from the people and will become a permanent, elite ruling class.

Farmer V

Opposes the lack of a Bill of Rights, stressing that explicit protections are necessary to safeguard individual liberties.

Farmer VI

Questions the effectiveness of the Constitution’s checks and balances, arguing they are insufficient to prevent abuses of power.

Farmer VII

Concerns about the potential for a strong national government to override state governments and harm local interests.

Farmer VIII

Warns against the creation of a standing army under federal control, fearing it could be used to subjugate the states and people.

Farmer IX

Believes that the new Constitution will lead to a powerful executive branch that could threaten republican values and liberty.

Farmer X

Argues the system proposed by the Constitution will lead to a national government that undermines the rights and powers of the states.

Farmer XI

Expresses concern that the absence of a Bill of Rights in the new Constitution would leave citizens vulnerable to government overreach.

Farmer XII

Critiques the centralization of power in the federal government, claiming it will diminish the importance of local governments and lead to corruption.

Farmer XIII

Fears that the federal judiciary will undermine the authority of state courts, leading to an imbalance of power and loss of rights for citizens.

Farmer XIV

Warns that the Senate will become too powerful and disconnected from the people, effectively creating an aristocracy.

Farmer XV

Criticizes the proposed method for electing senators, arguing it concentrates power in the hands of a few, sidelining the people’s influence.

Farmer XVI

Argues that the powers given to the federal government are too broad, threatening the liberties of the states and individuals.

Farmer XVII

Warns that the lack of term limits and the extensive powers of the federal government will lead to corruption and a permanent political elite.

Farmer XVIII

Calls for a more balanced system that preserves state sovereignty and limits federal overreach, stressing the need for a Bill of Rights.
