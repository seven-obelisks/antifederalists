The Federal Farmer

The Federal Farmer was the pseudonym used by an anonymous Anti-Federalist writer during the U.S. Constitution ratification debates. The authorship is often attributed to Richard Henry Lee, a Virginia statesman.

The Federal Farmer’s essays opposed the Constitution, arguing it would centralize power and weaken state authority. He criticized the lack of a Bill of Rights, fearing that without such protections, the new government could become tyrannical. The Federal Farmer called for amendments to safeguard individual liberties and maintain the balance of power between the states and the federal government.

The Federal Farmer I

Warns that the new Constitution grants excessive power to the national government, threatening state sovereignty, individual liberties, and creating the potential for tyranny.

The Federal Farmer II

Argues that the proposed government will be too distant from the people, leading to poor representation and weakening accountability.

The Federal Farmer III

Expresses concern that the Constitution’s expansive powers over taxation, military control, and the potential for a standing army will lead to federal overreach.

The Federal Farmer IV

Critiques the proposed Senate, suggesting it will be an undemocratic body disconnected from the people, which could lead to a permanent, powerful elite.

The Federal Farmer V

Opposes the lack of a Bill of Rights, stressing that explicit protections for individual freedoms are necessary to prevent government abuse and ensure liberties.

The Federal Farmer VI

Questions the effectiveness of the Constitution’s checks and balances, arguing they will be inadequate in preventing abuses of power and protecting individual rights.

The Federal Farmer VII

Concerns about the centralization of power in the federal government and the lack of sufficient limits on federal authority, particularly regarding state rights and sovereignty.

The Federal Farmer VIII

Raises alarm over the creation of a standing federal army, fearing it could be used to suppress state governments and the will of the people.

The Federal Farmer IX

Criticizes the proposed executive branch for its potential to become overly powerful, undermining the balance of power and threatening republican principles.

The Federal Farmer X

Argues that the centralization of power in the federal government would weaken the authority of state governments and harm the rights and interests of local communities.

The Federal Farmer XI

Expresses concern that the new Constitution fails to adequately protect individual rights by not including a Bill of Rights, which could leave citizens vulnerable to abuse of power.

The Federal Farmer XII

Warns that the centralization of military and civil power will allow the federal government to override state authority, threatening local autonomy and governance.

The Federal Farmer XIII

Critiques the potential for the federal government’s centralization of authority to lead to a tyrannical system with unchecked power, eroding state governments and individual freedoms.

The Federal Farmer XIV

Argues that the lack of clear provisions for regular elections and term limits in the proposed system will lead to an entrenched political class and foster corruption.

The Federal Farmer XV

Suggests the new system would concentrate too much power in the Senate and executive, weakening the House of Representatives and creating an imbalance in government.

The Federal Farmer XVI

Warns that the broad powers granted to the federal government in the Constitution would lead to a dangerous concentration of power, diminishing state authority and individual freedoms.

The Federal Farmer XVII

Expresses concern that the federal government’s growing powers would undermine state governments, rendering them irrelevant and stripping citizens of local representation.

The Federal Farmer XVIII

Calls for the rejection of the Constitution, arguing it grants too much centralized power, lacks sufficient protections for individual rights, and could lead to governmental tyranny.
