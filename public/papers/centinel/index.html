Centinel

Centinel was the pseudonym used by an Anti-Federalist writer during the U.S. Constitution ratification debates. The authorship is most often attributed to Samuel Bryan, a prominent lawyer and politician from Pennsylvania.

Centinel’s essays opposed the proposed Constitution, arguing it would create a national government with too much power, undermining state authority and individual freedoms. He warned that without a Bill of Rights, the new government could become tyrannical. Centinel emphasized the need for explicit protections for civil liberties to prevent abuses of power and preserve democratic values.

Centinel I

Warns that centralizing power in the federal government undermines state authority and individual freedoms.

Centinel II

Argues that a large republic cannot effectively protect the rights and interests of its citizens.

Centinel III

Rejects the need for a new constitution, arguing that state governments are capable of maintaining order and governance.

Centinel IV

Criticizes the proposed judiciary for granting the federal government excessive control over legal matters.

Centinel V

Highlights the lack of a Bill of Rights as a major oversight, leaving citizens vulnerable to government abuse.

Centinel VI

Explores how centralizing power erodes state autonomy and weakens local self-governance.

Centinel VII

Warns that federal taxation policies will unfairly burden the states, particularly those with agricultural economies.

Centinel VIII

Raises concerns about federal control of commerce, potentially disadvantaging local industries and empowering monopolies.

Centinel IX

Criticizes vague constitutional language, fearing it will allow the federal government to expand its power unchecked.

Centinel X

Calls for amending the Articles of Confederation rather than adopting a new, untested system.

Centinel XI

Urges rejection of the Constitution, arguing that its adoption would undermine liberty and state sovereignty.

Centinel XII

Argues that the new Constitution grants excessive power to the national government, threatening state sovereignty and individual liberties.

Centinel XII

Critiques the Senate’s structure as aristocratic, with unequal representation and concentrated power.

Centinel XIV

Rejects the notion that the Constitution’s checks and balances will prevent tyranny, fearing it will empower a central government.

Centinel XV

Criticizes the lack of a Bill of Rights, claiming it invites government overreach and threatens individual freedoms.

Centinel XVI

Warns that the strong federal judiciary would undermine state courts and give the national government undue influence over local matters.

Centinel XVII

Claims the Constitution would erode state legislatures’ powers, subordinating them to the federal government.

Centinel XVIII

Calls for the rejection of the Constitution, arguing it is fundamentally flawed and will lead to dangerous centralization.
